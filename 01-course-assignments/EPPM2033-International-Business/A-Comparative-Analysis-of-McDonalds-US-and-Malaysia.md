## Introduction

McDonald’s Corporation is one of the most visible examples of how a multinational enterprise can combine global standardization with local responsiveness. This report argues that the company’s sustained international success rests on a deliberate “glocal” strategy: protecting a consistent global brand identity while systematically adapting products, marketing messages, and operational practices to national cultural differences. Using concepts of globalization motives and Hofstede’s cultural dimensions introduced in the early chapters of the course textbook (Cavusgil et al., 2019), the analysis examines the company’s background, the drivers of its international expansion, cultural contrasts between the United States and Malaysia, concrete adaptations in both markets, and one practical recommendation for managing the tension between global efficiency and local relevance.

---

## 1. Company Overview

Ray Kroc did not start the restaurant. Richard and Maurice McDonald ran a small but successful drive-in in San Bernardino, California, selling burgers, fries and drinks from a short menu. Kroc visited in 1954 while selling Multimixers, founded McDonald’s System, Inc., a predecessor of McDonald’s Corp., in 1955, and six years later bought the exclusive rights to the McDonald’s name and operating system (McDonald’s Corporation, n.d.). Headquarters are now in Chicago (McDonald’s Corporation, 2025).

The system is large and mostly franchised: 45,356 restaurants in more than 100 countries at the end of 2025, about 95 percent of them run by franchisees, on systemwide sales near $139.4 billion (McDonald’s Corporation, 2025). Burgers, chicken, fries and drinks form the core menu, with local items added market by market. The United States remains the largest single market at roughly 13,700 restaurants, while Malaysia has more than 370 outlets and reports over 13.5 million customers a month (McDonald’s Malaysia, n.d.-a).

---

## 2. Globalization

Finding customers, rather than resources, drove the expansion. As the U.S. market neared saturation in the 1970s and 1980s, the company looked abroad for growing middle classes who wanted cheap, convenient meals (Cavusgil et al., 2019). Resource seeking barely figured: where it could, McDonald’s bought locally, which cut logistics costs and, in principle, eased relations with local suppliers. Efficiency came from the model: franchising and a standardized kitchen let the company spread purchasing and training costs over many more restaurants, and made brand-building cheaper per unit.

Entry became cheaper first. Trade barriers and restrictions on foreign direct investment fell from the 1980s (Cavusgil et al., 2019), and McDonald’s used the opening; its first Malaysian outlet appeared on Jalan Bukit Bintang in Kuala Lumpur in April 1982 (McDonald’s Malaysia, n.d.-b). Standard equipment, point-of-sale systems, then digital ordering and a loyalty app kept quality consistent as the chain spread. Competition from other global chains and local operators forced constant innovation and wider reach. Applied here, Levitt’s (1983) argument would predict converging needs and a victory for standardized products. McDonald’s only half bears that out. The company’s 2025 Form 10-K describes a menu that is broadly uniform but varies geographically to suit local preferences and tastes (McDonald’s Corporation, 2025); Malaysia sits in the variation half: Halal certification, a fried-chicken line, a borrowed nickname. Those changes alter what the product is, not how it is sold, and that is the stronger signal here.

## 3. Cultural Environment

Two scores carry most of the argument: individualism reaches 91 in the United States and 26 in Malaysia (Hofstede, 2011; Hofstede Insights, n.d.). They are national averages, not individuals, as Hofstede (2011) stresses, so reading 91 as “every American individualist” would misuse Table 1; as comparisons of typical expectations, they still explain why service and group dining differ.

American customers expect to choose what they order, and to get it quickly; Malaysians more often treat a meal as a group occasion with family or colleagues (Hofstede, 2011). Power distance runs the opposite way, 40 against 100, and service expectations follow: customers in Malaysia are more likely to accept formal, deferential interaction, while American settings expect something flatter (Hofstede, 2011).

Meals are social occasions as much as purchases: Malaysian meals are frequently shared (Hofstede, 2011), and Ramadan adds a seasonal layer: the Misi Moreh Mekdi program delivers *moreh* meals to mosques and suraus (McDonald’s Malaysia, n.d.-c). Festive periods are framed the same way, as occasions when families and friends gather to share meals (McDonald’s Malaysia, 2025). Elsewhere the pattern reverses, with personal convenience and on-the-go eating more visible in the United States (Hofstede, 2011). Language is a separate difference: English dominates in the U.S., while Malaysia is multilingual. All of these differences feed into menu design and the promotional calendar.

## 4. Cultural Adaptation

Glocalization, adapting a standard offering to local conditions without abandoning the global brand, is what the two cases below illustrate (Cavusgil et al., 2019).

U.S. stores solve for speed. In September 2024, Bloomberg reported that McDonald’s was rolling out a new format in the U.S. with digital ordering stations that can take cash and give change (Bloomberg, 2024), alongside drive-thru lanes and mobile ordering. Such formats suit a market where customers value autonomy and control over their time (Hofstede, 2011). The menu is where the company does not move: Big Mac, Quarter Pounder with Cheese, Filet-O-Fish and Chicken McNuggets keep the U.S. core close to uniform (McDonald’s Corporation, 2025).

Malaysia solves for permission. Religious rules decide what can be sold at all, and corporate milestones record JAKIM Halal certification in February 1995, with the company describing itself as the first quick-service restaurant in the country to obtain it (McDonald’s Malaysia, n.d.-b). Halal status means compliance with Islamic dietary rules; the community page lists Ramadan *moreh* distribution and business zakat contributions among its activities (McDonald’s Malaysia, n.d.-c). Local product development runs on its own track: Ayam Goreng McD was introduced in November 1987, and the Prosperity Burger was created and launched in Malaysia in 1994 (McDonald’s Malaysia, n.d.-b). A 2025 announcement promised RM0.20 to Ronald McDonald House Charities Malaysia for every Prosperity Burger meal bought between 20 November and 31 December 2025. It called the burger “proudly made in Malaysia since 1994” with its signature black pepper sauce, and described it as a tradition that has “brought families and friends together” (McDonald’s Malaysia, 2025).

The name “Mekdi” is not the company’s own invention; in 2019 it said it was embracing the nickname “Malaysians have affectionately given us” (Tan, 2019). That August, ahead of Merdeka and Malaysia Day, McDonald’s put up “Mekdi” signage at its Bukit Bintang outlet, the first McDonald’s to open in Malaysia, in 1982 (Tan, 2019; McDonald’s Malaysia, n.d.-b). Melati Abdul Hai, then vice president and chief marketing officer, described the sign as a commemoration of how beloved the brand had grown among Malaysians, and added: “Our business focuses on Malaysians and understanding their needs, whether through our menu items, charity efforts, or nurturing our talent” (Tan, 2019). Presenting itself as “a Malaysian company at heart” rather than a foreign one follows from the same move (Tan, 2019). Service stays polite and formal, consistent with high power distance, though public evidence on internal training is thin.

The two cases are not equally deep, though. Halal certification changes what the company may sell; a kiosk changes how it is sold. The evidence here suggests religious and collective norms are where standardization costs the most, which guides an adaptation budget better than the scores do.

---

## 5. Conclusion and Recommendation

The hard problem is holding global efficiency and local cultural and religious compliance at the same time. Push standardization too far and Malaysian customers who expect Halal integrity and local relevance drift away; push localization too far and the brand and the franchise model lose the consistency that keeps them cheap to run.

One concrete fix is a permanent cross-functional Local Innovation Board in each major market, co-chaired by the country managing director and a corporate brand representative. It would test menu and marketing proposals against power distance (how far hierarchical sign-off is accepted), collectivism (family and group appeal) and Halal integrity, plus global brand rules, and pilot before national rollout. Scorecards should track sales, Halal verification and customer feedback. Slower decisions are the obvious cost, and fixed timelines plus clear escalation rules contain it.

The board’s design also depends on culture. In Malaysia, senior chairs carry authority and are likely to be accepted (Hofstede, 2011); in the United States the same structure may meet resistance and need more participatory process before it is seen as legitimate. Authority is the weak point. A board without veto power becomes one more review layer that local managers learn to route around; a board with veto power can stall a rollout. Which risk McDonald’s Malaysia prefers to carry will settle whether the structure protects cultural fit or only adds delay.
